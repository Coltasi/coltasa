Colin Constantine, Principal{{ post.date }}{{ post.mins }}

It is the last week of the quarter. The deal is late, the forecast is short, and the buyer has just asked for "a bit more flexibility on price". The seller offers fifteen percent off to get it signed by Friday. The buyer agrees. Everyone is relieved.

Then next quarter, the same buyer waits until the last week again. So does their procurement team at renewal. And the sellers who watched it happen learn that discounting is how deals close.

Discounts given away for nothing are the most expensive habit in B2B sales. They cut revenue, they train buyers to wait, and they signal that your price was never real. The alternative is simple to describe and hard to practise: never give a discount, trade it.

Why buyers ask

Most buyers ask for a discount because it is their job to ask, not because the deal depends on it. Procurement teams are often measured on savings. Asking costs them nothing. If the answer is an instant yes, they learn the first number was padded, and they push for more.

So the first rule is not to treat the request as a crisis. It is a normal part of the process, and you should plan for it before it arrives.

Prepare before you negotiate

  • Know your walk-away point. Decide in advance the lowest price and worst terms you will accept, and get them approved internally.
  • List your tradeables. Write down what you could give and what you want in return. Concessions are only useful if you have planned them.
  • Know their alternative. What happens if they do not buy from you? A competitor, building in-house, or doing nothing. The weaker their alternative, the stronger your position.
  • Anchor on value early. If the buyer agreed the problem is worth solving and the cost of doing nothing is high, price becomes one part of a bigger conversation.

The rule: if, then

Every concession should be conditional. Phrase it as: "If you can do this, then we can do that." It keeps the exchange balanced, and it tests whether the request is serious. Useful things to ask for in return:

  • A longer contract term, such as three years instead of one.
  • Payment upfront or annually in advance.
  • A larger initial commitment or more seats.
  • A signed case study, a reference call or a logo on your site.
  • Signature by a specific date, agreed in the mutual success plan.
  • Removing redlines from your standard contract.

If the buyer will not give anything in return, the discount was not necessary to close the deal.

Concede slowly and in shrinking steps

When you do move, move in decreasing amounts: a larger first step, a smaller second, a very small third. It signals you are approaching your limit. Large or growing concessions tell the buyer there is plenty left. And never split the difference by reflex. It rewards whoever started furthest away.

Stop the end-of-quarter cycle

Most discounting happens because the seller needs the deal more than the buyer does, and the buyer knows it. The fix starts long before the negotiation:

  • Build a mutual success plan anchored to the buyer's deadline, not your quarter.
  • Surface procurement, legal and security early so they do not land in the last week.
  • Keep enough pipeline that no single deal feels like it must close at any price.
  • Give sellers a clear discount approval process so they cannot give away margin under pressure.

Be willing to say no

The strongest position in any negotiation is being genuinely willing to walk away. That does not mean being difficult. It means being clear about your value and calm about your limits. Buyers respect sellers who hold their price for good reasons. They rarely respect the ones who fold.

Protecting price is not about winning a negotiation. It is about building a business where your customers pay what the product is worth, and your renewals do not start with an argument.
