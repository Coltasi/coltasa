# Writing a cold email a senior buyer will read

URL: https://www.coltasa.com/insights/cold-email-senior-buyers  
Author: Colin Constantine  
Published: 2026-10-05  
Category: Outbound

> A practical guide to short, specific outbound emails for enterprise buyers, with a simple structure and what to leave out.

Photo: [Vitaly Gariev](https://www.pexels.com/photo/elderly-businessman-using-smartphone-in-office-30535783) on Pexels
Senior buyers receive dozens of sales emails a day, and most are deleted after the first line. The ones that get a reply usually have a few things in common: they are short, they are clearly written for that person, and they ask for very little. The bar keeps rising: in Gartner's 2026 survey, [67 percent of B2B buyers said they prefer a rep-free buying experience](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience), so an email has to earn the conversation.

## Research first

A good email starts before you write anything. Spend ten minutes on the account and the person. Look for something specific and recent, such as a new initiative, a hiring push, a product launch, a change in leadership or something they said publicly. You need one real reason to write to this person now.

## A simple structure

Most effective cold emails fit into four parts and under 100 words:

- Why them: one sentence on what you noticed and why it matters.

- The problem: one sentence on a challenge companies like theirs often face at this point.

- The proof: one sentence on how you helped a similar company, with a specific result.

- The ask: a low-effort question, such as whether this is a priority this year.

## An example

"Hi Sarah, I saw that you are moving three brands onto one ecommerce platform this year. Teams doing that often find content launches slow down for a few months during the migration. We helped a similar retailer cut launch time in half during their move. Is speeding up launches something you are looking at this year?"

It is specific, it is about her, and the question is easy to answer.

## What to leave out

- Opening lines about yourself or your company.

- Compliments that could apply to anyone.

- Lists of features.

- A request for 30 minutes in the first email.

- Attachments and multiple links.

## Subject lines

Keep subject lines short and plain, a few words that relate to the content. "Brand migration" or "Content launches" works better than something clever. The aim is to look like a normal work email.

## Follow up with something new

Most replies come after the first email, so plan three or four messages over two or three weeks. Each follow-up should add something, such as a relevant article, a short observation about their market or a different angle on the problem. Avoid messages that only ask whether they saw the last one.

## Use more than email

Email works best alongside other touches. A LinkedIn connection request with a short note, [a handwritten card](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/signal-over-noise) or an introduction from a mutual contact all make your name familiar before or after the email arrives.

## Measure the right things

Track replies and meetings booked with the right people, not opens or emails sent. If a message is not working after 30 or 40 sends to well-chosen accounts, change the angle rather than the volume. Volume also carries a cost: Google's [sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/14229414?hl=en-GB) ask senders to keep reported spam below 0.1 percent, and every ignored, generic email makes that harder. A small number of well-researched emails will usually outperform a large number of generic ones. When someone does reply, the next job is [a first call that earns the second meeting](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/discovery-call).
