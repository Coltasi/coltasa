# When to stop selling and hand it to your first rep

> How to know your B2B startup is ready to hand off founder-led sales, who to hire first, and a three-stage handoff that keeps deals moving.

Photo: [RDNE Stock project](https://www.pexels.com/photo/hands-exchanging-keys-10375957) on Pexels
Founders should hand off sales once they can win deals the same way more than once, can write down how, and are spending so much time selling that the product or company is suffering. That point usually arrives somewhere between your first five and fifteen paying customers. The handoff itself should take a quarter, not a week, and the founder should stay in the deal room as an executive sponsor long after they stop running deals.

Most founders get the timing roughly right and the handoff wrong. They hire a seller, give them a login and a list of leads, and step away. Six months later the pipeline has dried up and the conclusion is that the hire was bad. Often the hire was fine. The handoff was the problem.

## When should you stop doing founder-led sales?

You are ready to hand off when the evidence says your sales motion is repeatable, not when you are tired of selling. Look for these signs:

- You have won several deals with a similar buyer, a similar problem and a similar path to signature.
- You can explain why you win and why you lose without referring to who you know.
- Deals are coming from more than your personal network, through referrals, inbound interest or outbound you did not send yourself.
- Selling is taking time you need for product, hiring or fundraising, and you can see it in what is not getting done.
If the first two are missing, hiring a seller will not fix it. A new rep cannot discover your market for you. They can only run a motion that already exists. That is why it pays to [pick one segment](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/pick-one-segment) and win it before you hire anyone.

## What should you have in place before the first sales hire starts?

Before day one, write down what you have been doing by instinct. It does not need to be polished. It needs to be specific enough that someone else could follow it. At minimum:

- Who you sell to: the type of company, the team and the job title that signs.
- The problem you solve for them, in the buyer's words, taken from real calls.
- The stages a deal goes through and what has to be true to move from one to the next.
- The three or four objections you hear most, and how you answer them.
- Pricing, discount limits and who approves exceptions.
- A list of every closed deal with a short note on how it was won.
This is the start of a playbook, and it is the most useful thing you can give a new seller. There is more on what to include in [writing the playbook before you hire the team](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/first-sales-playbook).

## Who should your first sales hire be?

Hire a builder who can sell, not a manager who used to. Your first seller will work without a brand, without a full product and without a team around them. They need to be comfortable running a deal end to end, including the parts that bigger companies hand to someone else, such as qualifying their own leads and writing their own proposals.

Many founders hire two sellers at once so they can compare results. That is reasonable advice, because one hire on their own leaves you unable to tell whether a bad quarter is the person or the motion. If budget allows only one, set clear early milestones so you can judge the motion separately from the individual.

Avoid hiring a VP of Sales first. A senior leader needs a working motion and a team to lead. Without either, they will spend their time trying to build both, and you will pay a leadership salary for individual work.

Not everyone agrees on timing. [SaaStr’s guide to the transition](https://www.saastr.com/the-founders-guide-to-transitioning-from-founder-led-sales-why-most-get-it-wrong-and-how-to-get-it-right) argues founders should stay hands-on for much longer, in some cases until around $10M in annual recurring revenue, with a few account executives first and a head of sales later. The common ground is the sequence: sellers before a sales leader, and the founder still in the most important deals.

## How do you hand off deals without losing momentum?

Hand off in three stages over roughly one quarter, moving to the next stage only when the current one is working.

- Shadow. The new seller joins your calls and takes notes. After each call, spend ten minutes on why you asked what you asked and what you would do next. This is where the unwritten parts of your process come out.
- Co-sell. The seller runs the calls and you join. Agree beforehand which parts are theirs. Resist the urge to take over when a call gets difficult. Debrief afterwards instead.
- Lead. The seller owns the deals. You join only for specific moments: an executive meeting, a pricing conversation or a deal at risk. The seller decides when to bring you in.
Hand over new opportunities first. Deals that are already deep in a relationship with you can stay with you to close, with the seller alongside. Moving a buyer to a new contact late in a deal is one of the easiest ways to lose it.

## What role should the founder keep after the handoff?

The founder stops running deals but stays the most senior voice in them. Enterprise buyers want to meet the person who built the company, especially early on. Keep three jobs:

- Executive sponsor on large or strategic deals, meeting your counterpart at the buyer once or twice per deal.
- The weekly [deal review](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/forecast-evidence), where you ask questions about the evidence behind each deal rather than taking it over.
- The feedback loop to product, so what sellers hear from buyers reaches the people building the product.
What you give up is control of the calendar and the first call. That is the point.

## How do you know the handoff is working?

Judge the handoff on leading indicators in the first quarter, not on closed revenue. Enterprise sales cycles are long enough that a new seller may not close anything they sourced for months. Watch instead for:

- Meetings booked and held each week against a target you agreed up front.
- How many first meetings turn into qualified opportunities, compared with your own rate when you were selling.
- Deals moving between stages on time, with the exit criteria met.
- Whether the seller can explain each deal: who the buyer is, why they would buy and what happens next.
If these look healthy, revenue usually follows. If they do not, look at the motion and the handoff before you look at the person.

## What to do this week

Write a one-page summary of how your last five deals were won: who bought, why, how long it took and what nearly stopped it. If you can write it easily, you are close to ready to hire. If you cannot, that page is your next priority, because it is the first thing your new seller will need.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many customers should a founder close before hiring a salesperson?

There is no fixed number, but most B2B founders are ready somewhere between five and fifteen paying customers. What matters more than the count is whether those deals look alike: a similar buyer, a similar problem and a similar path to signature. If every deal was different, keep selling until a pattern appears.

### Should my first sales hire be a VP of Sales?

Usually not. A VP of Sales needs a working sales motion and a team to lead. Your first hire should be an experienced individual seller who is comfortable building process as they go. Hire a sales leader once you have a few sellers hitting their numbers and need someone to scale what works.

### How long should the transition from founder-led sales take?

Plan for about one quarter. Move through three stages: the new seller shadows your calls, then runs calls with you present, then owns deals and brings you in only for key moments. Move on from each stage when it is working, not on a fixed date.

### Should founders stay involved in sales after hiring a sales team?

Yes, as an executive sponsor rather than the person running deals. Founders should meet senior buyers on large or strategic deals, lead or attend the weekly deal review, and make sure what sellers hear from customers reaches the product team.
