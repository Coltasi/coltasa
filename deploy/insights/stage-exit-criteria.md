# If the buyer has not done it, the deal has not moved

> How to write sales stage exit criteria your team will use: two or three buyer actions per stage, each with evidence you can check in the CRM, inspected weekly.

Photo: [RDNE Stock project](https://www.pexels.com/photo/black-pen-on-white-pad-paper-8580716) on Pexels
Sales stage exit criteria are the conditions a deal must meet before it can move to the next stage of your pipeline. The ones your team will actually use are short, written as things the buyer has done rather than things the seller has done, and checkable by anyone who opens the record. If a criterion needs a conversation to interpret, it is not finished.

Most early sales teams have stages. Far fewer have exit criteria that mean anything. The result is a pipeline where "proposal" means a proposal was sent and "commit" means a rep feels good. This post covers how to write criteria that fix that, with an example set you can adapt this week.

## What are sales stage exit criteria?

An exit criterion is a verifiable condition that must be true before a deal leaves a stage. A stage tells you where a deal is. Exit criteria tell you what has to happen for it to move, and what evidence proves it has.

They matter because stage is the main input to almost every forecast. If stages are reached on activity ("demo done", "proposal sent"), your forecast measures how busy the team is, not how close buyers are to signing. That is a large part of why confidence is low: in a [Gartner survey of sales leaders and sellers](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2020-02-12-gartner-says-less-than-50--of-sales-leaders-and-selle), only 45 percent had high confidence in their organisation's forecasting accuracy, and Gartner named poor data quality as one of the main causes. Vague stages are poor data with a progress bar on top.

If you have already moved to [a forecast built on evidence rather than optimism](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/forecast-evidence), exit criteria are how you make that evidence consistent from one rep to the next.

## Why do most exit criteria get ignored?

Exit criteria get ignored when they are long, vague or impossible to check. A team that inherits a twelve-point checklist per stage will tick the boxes it can and skip the rest, and nobody will notice because nobody can tell the difference.

The usual failures are:

- Seller activity instead of buyer action. "Sent proposal" can be true in a deal that is going nowhere. "Buyer reviewed the proposal and asked for changes" cannot.
- Words that need interpreting. "Strong interest", "good relationship" and "budget likely" mean different things to every rep.
- Too many criteria. More than three or four per stage and reps stop reading them.
- No evidence required. If the CRM only asks for a tick, you are recording opinion.
- Nobody inspects them. Criteria that are never checked in a pipeline review become decoration within a month.

## How do you write an exit criterion your team will use?

Write each criterion as a buyer action with a named person and a piece of evidence you could show a sceptical colleague. That single rule removes most of the ambiguity.

Test every criterion against five questions before it goes into your playbook:

- Is it something the buyer did, said or agreed, rather than something we did?
- Is it binary? Two people looking at the same deal should reach the same answer.
- Can the evidence live in the CRM: a field, a date, a document, a named contact?
- Would losing this condition make us less confident the deal will close? If not, cut it.
- Can a rep explain it in one sentence without looking it up?Keep it to two or three criteria per stage. You are not trying to describe everything a good seller does. You are describing the minimum proof that the buyer has moved.

## What do good exit criteria look like? An example for enterprise sales

Here is an illustrative set for a five-stage enterprise process. Adapt the wording to your product and buyer, but keep the shape: every line is a buyer action with evidence.

- Discovery to Qualified. The buyer has described a problem in their own words and its cost to the business. You know who else is involved in the decision, by name. A second meeting is booked.
- Qualified to Solution fit. A champion has agreed to help and has introduced at least one other stakeholder. The buyer has confirmed the success criteria they will use to judge a solution.
- Solution fit to Business case. The economic buyer has seen the business case, built on the buyer's own numbers. A budget range or funding route has been named.
- Business case to Commercials. The buyer has agreed a target signature date and the steps to get there, ideally in a [mutual success plan](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/mutual-success-plans). Procurement, legal and security contacts are known.
- Commercials to Closed won. Terms are agreed in writing and the signatory and signing date are confirmed by the buyer.Notice what is missing: demos given, emails sent, calls held. Those are inputs. They belong in your activity data, not in your stage definitions.

Notice too that probability follows evidence. If your win rate from the Business case stage is, say, 40 percent over the last year, use that figure for the stage rather than a number someone picked when the CRM was set up. Revisit it each quarter.

## How do you get your sales team to actually use exit criteria?

Make the criteria visible where reps work, make the evidence required at the moment a deal moves, and inspect them every week. Training on its own will not do it.

- Put them in the CRM, at the stage. Salesforce's Path feature lets admins [highlight key fields and add guidance for success](https://help.salesforce.com/apex/HTViewHelpDoc?id=path_overview.htm) at each stage, so the criteria sit on the record the rep is updating.
- Require the evidence when the stage changes. HubSpot pipelines can [show and require specific properties](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/object-settings/set-up-and-customize-pipelines) when a user moves a deal into a stage. Use that for the one or two fields that prove the criterion, such as the champion's name or the agreed signature date.
- Inspect them in the forecast call. When a deal moves forward, ask "what did the buyer do?" If the answer is not on the record, the deal moves back. A [30-minute weekly forecast call](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/weekly-forecast-call) is the right place for this.
- Write them with the team. Draft the criteria, then test them against ten recent deals with your reps. Where people disagree about whether a deal met a criterion, the wording is not clear enough yet.
- Review them every quarter. Look at deals that were lost or slipped from late stages and ask which criterion should have caught them.Expect stages to go backwards for the first few weeks. That is the point. A pipeline that shrinks because it is now honest is more useful than one that looks healthy and misses.

## When should you change your stages or exit criteria?

Change them when the data tells you a stage is not predicting anything. If deals pass a stage and then close at roughly the same rate as deals in the stage before, the criterion is not separating good deals from bad ones and needs to be tightened.

Change them, too, when your buyer changes: a move upmarket usually adds security review, procurement and legal steps that need their own evidence. Otherwise, resist the urge to tinker. Criteria that change every month cannot be compared over time, and comparison is what makes them useful for forecasting.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many exit criteria should each sales stage have?

Two or three is usually enough. Each criterion should be a buyer action with evidence you can check in the CRM. Long checklists get skimmed and ticked without thought, so a short list that is enforced every week will improve your forecast far more than a comprehensive one that nobody reads or inspects.

### What is the difference between entry criteria and exit criteria?

Entry criteria describe what must be true for a deal to enter a stage, and exit criteria describe what must be true for it to leave. In practice they are the same conditions seen from either side, so most teams write only exit criteria. That keeps one definition per stage boundary and avoids two lists drifting apart over time.

### Should exit criteria be enforced in the CRM?

Enforce the one or two pieces of evidence that matter most, such as the champion's name or an agreed signature date, by making those fields required when a deal moves stage. Do not lock down everything. Too many required fields push reps to enter placeholder values, which makes the data look complete while making it less accurate.

### How do exit criteria improve forecast accuracy?

They make stage a measure of buyer progress rather than seller activity. When every deal in a stage has met the same verifiable conditions, the historical win rate for that stage becomes meaningful, and you can use it to weight the pipeline. Without consistent criteria, two deals in the same stage can be at completely different points.
