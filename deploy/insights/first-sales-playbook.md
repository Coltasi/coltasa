# Write the playbook before you hire the team

Author: Colin Constantine  
Published: 2026-10-05  
Category: Strategy

> Hiring sellers before you can explain how your deals are won is the most expensive mistake in early-stage sales. Here is what to write down first.

Photo: [Yen Vu](https://unsplash.com/photos/an-open-notebook-filled-with-handwritten-text-3NXtW3eXI2k) on Unsplash
A familiar pattern: a company raises its round, the founder has closed the first twenty customers personally, and the board wants growth. So the company hires three account executives. Six months later, one is doing fine, one is struggling and one has left. Pipeline is flat and nobody can say exactly why.

The problem is rarely the people. It is that the company hired sellers before it could explain how its deals are won. The founder knew, but the knowledge lived in their head. A playbook gets it out.

## What a playbook is, and is not

A playbook is not a 60-page document nobody reads. It is a short, practical description of who you sell to, why they buy, how a deal moves from first conversation to signature, and what good looks like at each step. A new seller should be able to read it in an afternoon and run a credible first call the next day.

It also does not need to be perfect. Version one will be partly wrong. That is fine, because a written process can be tested and improved. One that lives in the founder's head cannot.

## The six sections to write first

1. Ideal customer profile. Describe the companies that buy fastest, pay the most and stay the longest. Use real data from your customer list: industry, size, region, tech stack, and the trigger that made them start looking. Be specific enough that a seller can rule an account out. If the list points to several groups, [pick one segment and win it first](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/pick-one-segment).

2. Buyer personas. For each role involved in a decision, write down what they care about, what they are measured on and what they worry about. The economic buyer, the champion and the technical evaluator usually want different things from the same conversation.

3. The problems you solve. List the three to five problems customers bring to you, in their words, not yours. For each, note the questions that uncover it and the cost of leaving it unsolved. This becomes the backbone of discovery.

4. Sales stages with exit criteria. Define each stage of a deal by what the buyer has done, not what the seller has done. "Demo given" is a seller activity. "Champion has confirmed the problem, budget range and decision process" is evidence. Every stage needs a clear test for moving on. There is more on this in [a forecast is evidence, not optimism](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/forecast-evidence).

5. Proof. Collect the case studies, numbers and references that move deals forward, and note which persona each one works best with. New sellers waste weeks hunting for these.

6. Objections and answers. Write down the ten objections you hear most and how the best answers sound. Include competitive positioning: who you meet most often, where you win and where you should walk away.

## How to build it in a month

- Week 1: interview five recent customers about why they bought, and review your last ten wins and losses.

- Week 2: have someone shadow or record the founder on three live deals and write down the questions and moves that work.

- Week 3: draft the six sections. Keep each to one or two pages.

- Week 4: test the draft on live deals. Note where it is wrong, and fix it.

## Pick a methodology, then adapt it

You do not need to invent a sales process. Frameworks like [MEDDPICC](https://www.meddicc.com/meddic), [Sandler](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/sandler-sales-methodology/) and [Challenger](https://challengerinc.com/what-is-challenger-sales-methodology/) exist because they work. Choose one that fits how your buyers make decisions, then adapt the language to your product. Enterprise deals with large buying committees usually benefit from MEDDPICC's rigour on qualification. Shorter, more transactional sales may need something lighter. What matters most is that the whole team uses the same language.

## Then hire

With a playbook in place, hiring gets easier, and you can [hand off deals in stages](https://www.coltasa.com/insights/founder-led-sales-handoff) rather than all at once. You know which skills your deals need, so you can write a sharper job description and test for those skills in interviews. Onboarding drops from months to weeks. And when a new seller struggles, you can see exactly which stage is the problem.

Keep the playbook alive. Review it every quarter with the team, update it with what you have learned, and retire what no longer works. The best playbooks are written by the sellers who use them.
