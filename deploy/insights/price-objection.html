"It is too expensive" is a question, not an answer

How to handle price objections in enterprise sales: find what the buyer is comparing you with, rebuild the value case, then trade terms instead of discounting.

Colin Constantine, PrincipalOctober 20266 min read

When an enterprise buyer says "it is too expensive", they are rarely telling you the price is the problem. Usually the value is not yet clear to the people who have to approve it, the cost is being compared with the wrong thing, or the buyer is testing whether your number is real. To handle a price objection, do not defend the price or cut it. Ask what it is being compared with, rebuild the value case with the buyer, and only then talk about the shape of the deal.

The instinct of most sellers is to reach for a discount. That treats the objection as a statement of fact, when it is really a question. Your job is to find out which question is being asked.

What does "it is too expensive" actually mean?

"It is too expensive" almost always means one of five things, and each needs a different response. Before you answer, work out which one you are hearing.

  • The value is not proven. The buyer cannot yet connect your price to an outcome they care about. This is the most common meaning, and it is a discovery problem, not a pricing one.
  • The comparison is wrong. They are setting your price against a cheaper tool that does half the job, against last year's budget line, or against doing nothing.
  • The budget is genuinely short. The value is accepted but the money is not there this year, or not in this department.
  • It is a negotiating move. Asking costs nothing, and many buyers and procurement teams are measured on savings.
  • It is a polite no. They have decided not to proceed and price is the easiest reason to give.

The first two are the ones you can fix by selling better. The third needs creativity on terms. The fourth needs a calm, prepared position. The fifth needs honesty, and the sooner you find it the better for your forecast.

How do you respond to a price objection in the moment?

Respond with a question, not a justification. Acknowledge what they said, then find out what sits behind it. Defending the number straight away makes you sound nervous, and discounting straight away tells the buyer the first number was padded.

Four questions do most of the work:

  1. "Too expensive compared with what?" This surfaces the comparison, which is often the real issue.
  2. "If price were not a factor, is this the solution you would choose?" A hesitant answer tells you the objection is about fit or risk, not money.
  3. "Is it the total, the timing, or how it is structured?" This separates a value problem from a budget or cash flow problem.
  4. "Who else will look at this number, and what will they need to see?" This tells you who you still have to convince.

Then stop talking and listen. Write down the exact words. The answer decides whether the next conversation is about value, budget or terms.

How do you reframe price as value?

Reframe price by making the cost of the problem bigger and clearer than the cost of the solution, in the buyer's own numbers. This is where the work you did in a good discovery call pays off. If you never quantified the problem, you cannot do it now with confidence.

Take an illustrative example. Say your platform costs 120,000 a year. Your buyer told you in discovery that a manual process ties up four people for a third of their week, and that errors in it delay invoicing by around ten days each month. Do the arithmetic with them, using their salary and cash figures rather than yours. If the time alone is worth more than your price, the conversation changes from "is this expensive" to "is this the best use of the money".

The most important comparison is often doing nothing. Forrester's breakdown of deals lost to no decision starts with "You lose to status quo". A buyer who says your price is too high may be comparing it with the free option of carrying on as they are. Show what carrying on costs: in time, in risk, in revenue delayed. If that cost is small, the objection may be correct, and you should qualify harder rather than argue.

Who needs to believe the value, not just hear it?

The person raising the price objection is often not the person who has to approve the spend. Your champion may accept the value case completely and still lose the argument with finance. So the value case has to be written down, in terms a CFO would recognise, and owned by someone inside the business.

This is where a strong champion matters most. Build the numbers with them, not for them, so they can defend them in a room you are not in. Ask them what finance usually challenges and prepare for it. If the economic buyer has never heard from you directly, a price objection late in the deal is a sign that you are single-threaded and need to widen your contacts.

Buyers do want help here. A 2026 Gartner survey of 645 B2B buyers found that "Sixty-nine percent of B2B buyers prefer to validate AI-generated insights with sales reps". Buyers arrive with more research than ever, often including rough price comparisons, and they look to the seller for context and decision support. A clear, honest value case is exactly that.

When should you change the price, and how?

Change the commercial terms only when the value is accepted and a real constraint remains, and always get something back. A concession given for nothing teaches the buyer to ask again. A concession traded for something of value keeps your price credible. There is more on this in never give a discount, trade it.

Before you move on price, look at the shape of the deal. Many budget objections are really timing or structure objections:

  • Reduce scope, not rate. Start with fewer users, one team or one region, at your standard price, with an agreed path to expand.
  • Phase the payments. Align billing with their budget cycle or with the point at which value starts.
  • Trade for commitment. A longer term, a faster signature, a case study or a reference call are all worth something to you.
  • Trade for a pilot with exit criteria. A short, paid proof with agreed success measures moves the risk off the buyer without cutting the price.

Decide your walk-away point and your tradeable items before the negotiation, not during it. If procurement is involved, expect a formal request for a reduction whatever you do, and plan for it as described in working with procurement.

How do you prevent price objections before they happen?

You prevent most price objections by talking about money early and by agreeing the value before you show the number. A price that arrives as a surprise at the end of an evaluation will almost always be called too high.

  • Ask about budget, and how similar purchases were approved, in the first or second meeting.
  • Give a price range once you understand the scope, so nobody anchors on a figure from a competitor's website.
  • Agree the measurable outcome and its value in writing before the proposal.
  • Put the approval steps and owners in a mutual success plan.
  • Present the proposal live, starting with the problem and its cost, never by email with the price on page one.

If you do all of this and the price is still too high for a whole category of customer, take that seriously. It may be a signal about your target segment or your packaging rather than your selling.

Frequently asked questions

Should you ever offer a discount when a buyer says it is too expensive?

Only after the value is agreed and a genuine constraint remains, and only in exchange for something you value, such as a longer term, a faster signature, a reference or a larger scope. Offering a discount as the first response tells the buyer your price was never firm, invites further requests and lowers the starting point for every renewal that follows.

What is the best first question to ask after a price objection?

Ask what the price is being compared with. The answer tells you whether the buyer is benchmarking against a cheaper competitor, an existing budget line or doing nothing at all. Each comparison needs a different response, and most sellers skip this step and start defending a number before they know what it is up against.

How do you tell a negotiating tactic from a real budget problem?

Ask whether, with price aside, this is the solution they would choose, and whether the issue is the total, the timing or the structure. A buyer with a real budget problem will usually explain the constraint and engage with options such as phasing or reduced scope. A buyer using a tactic tends to repeat the request without detail.

Who should own the value case in an enterprise deal?

The seller builds it, but a champion inside the buyer's organisation should own it. Build the numbers together using the buyer's own figures for time, cost and risk, so the champion can defend them with finance and the economic buyer in meetings you will not attend. A value case only the seller believes rarely survives approval.
